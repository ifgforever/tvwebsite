Rule one: get the width right

This is the mistake we see most, and the one you can’t fix after delivery. A console narrower than the TV makes the screen look like it’s sliding off a ledge, and no amount of careful hanging rescues it.

A TV’s cabinet measures roughly 87% of its diagonal, so the screen is always wider than the number on the box suggests. Buy to these and you won’t regret it:

55″about 48″55″+

Going wider buys you something structural as well as visual: a 71-inch unit reaches four studs on standard 16-inch centres where a 47-inch one reaches three. That’s the difference between a console you can lean on and one you tiptoe around.

Rule two: check how it fixes to the wall

Every listing quotes a weight capacity, and every one of those numbers assumes the unit is bolted into structure. Into studs, 100 to 150 lb is real. Into drywall alone, treat anything over about 30 lb as a slow-motion accident whatever the box says.

So the useful question isn’t the rating, it’s the hardware. Three designs turn up:

A French cleat, or a steel rail

The best of the three, and what most good consoles use. A continuous strip screwed to the wall that the unit drops onto, so we fix into whatever framing is actually there rather than wherever the factory drilled.

Keyhole slots in the back panel

Workable, but the spacing is fixed and rarely lines up with your studs. Usually solved with a backing board behind the unit, which is a normal part of the job rather than a problem.

Through-holes in the back panel

The most flexible, as long as the panel is solid. If the back flexes when you press it, the wall isn’t the weak point — the furniture is, and no fixing saves it.

Common questions

How wide should a floating TV stand be?

At least as wide as the TV, and ideally wider. A TV's cabinet measures roughly 87% of its diagonal, so a 65-inch TV is about 57 inches wide and wants a 63-inch or wider console under it. A stand narrower than the screen makes the TV look like it is sliding off a ledge.

How much weight can a floating TV stand hold?

The number in the listing assumes it is bolted into studs. Fixed into framing, most floating consoles genuinely take 100 to 150 lb. Fixed into drywall alone, treat anything over about 30 lb as unsafe whatever the box claims. The limit is the wall, not the shelf.

What should I look for in how it mounts to the wall?

A French cleat or a steel mounting rail is the best of the three common designs, because you fix into whatever studs you find rather than wherever the manufacturer drilled. Keyhole slots and through-holes in the back panel both work too, as long as the back panel is solid rather than thin hardboard.
