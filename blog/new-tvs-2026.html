NEW TVS COMING IN 2026 — WHAT'S ACTUALLY WORTH KNOWING

Every brand puts out a "new lineup" every year. Most of it is marketing noise. Here's what's genuinely different in 2026, and what it actually means once that TV is out of the box and needs to get on your wall.

What's Actually New This Year

A few things in the 2026 lineups are real upgrades, not just a new model number on last year's panel.

  • Micro RGB is the big one. Samsung, LG, Hisense, and TCL are all pushing versions of it — individual red, green, and blue LEDs instead of a white backlight with a color filter. Brighter, more accurate color, and it's showing up in genuinely massive sizes (Samsung showed a 130-inch Micro RGB set at CES this year).
  • Big screens got a lot more affordable. 85-inch and larger TVs used to be a luxury purchase. Panel prices have dropped enough that 85"+ is now a realistic upgrade for a lot of living rooms, not just home theater rooms.
  • AI features are everywhere. Samsung's Vision AI Companion, conversational voice assistants, real-time upscaling that makes older content look sharper on a big panel. Useful for some people, ignorable for others — but it's now standard, not a premium add-on.
  • Refresh rates climbed. 144Hz–165Hz is becoming the norm on higher-end sets, mainly aimed at gamers, but it also just makes fast-motion sports look better.

None of this means last year's TV is obsolete. If you're happy with what you have, you're not missing much. But if you've been holding off on an upgrade, this is a genuinely good year to stop waiting.

The Part Nobody Mentions in the Reviews

Tech reviewers cover picture quality and specs. Nobody covers what happens after the box gets delivered — and this year, that part actually matters more than usual.

These TVs are getting bigger and heavier. An 85-inch or 98-inch panel isn't just "the same TV, more inches" — it's a meaningfully heavier load that needs to be on a stud (or a properly rated anchor), with a mount rated for that weight and size, installed level on the first try. A mount that was fine for a 55-inch set isn't automatically fine for a 98-inch one.

New panel types can be more particular to handle. QD-OLED and Micro RGB panels are not cheap to replace if something goes wrong during the mount — a dropped or flexed panel is a different kind of expensive mistake than it was with an older LCD set.

None of that is a reason to be nervous about upgrading. It's a reason to make sure whoever's putting it on the wall knows what they're doing.
