HOW HIGH SHOULD A TV BE MOUNTED? THE HEIGHT CHART

Almost nobody puts a TV on the wall for the look alone. They put it there to watch movies and shows — and height is the single thing that decides whether that's comfortable.

The Rule: Middle Of The Screen At Seated Eye Level

There's one number that matters, and it isn't a height off the floor — it's where your eyes are.

Sit on the couch you actually own, in the spot you actually sit, and look straight ahead. That line is where the middle of the screen belongs. For a standard sofa and an adult of average height, seated eye level lands somewhere around 40 to 44 inches from the floor. We use 42″ as the working number and adjust from there.

Notice what the rule is not about. It says nothing about the bottom of the TV, nothing about centering the screen on the wall, and nothing about lining it up with the mantel. Every one of those is a decorating instinct, and each one tends to push the TV higher than it should be.

The reason the rule works is boring and physical: your neck is comfortable looking straight ahead or slightly down, and it complains about being tipped back and held there. A movie is two hours. A season of something is a lot longer than that.

That's the target. It is not, in practice, where most TVs end up — because there's usually furniture in the way. More on that below, because it's the part the height charts always leave out.

The Chart: Where The Bottom Of Your TV Goes

You can't measure to the middle of a TV that's still in the box, so here's the same rule converted into a number you can mark on the wall. Take 42″ and subtract half the screen's height:

Screen Size Screen Height Bottom Of TV, From Floor
55″about 27″28″
65″about 32″26″
75″about 37″24″
85″about 42″21″

Two things people find surprising in that table.

The bigger the TV, the lower it hangs. That looks backwards until you remember the middle of the screen is staying put at eye level — a taller screen simply reaches further down from it. An 85″ mounted at the same height as a 55″ is a screen you'll be craning at.

These numbers are lower than most people expect. The bottom of a 75″ TV at 24″ off the floor sits at about the height of a dining chair seat. It feels low while you're holding the tape measure standing up. Sit down before you judge it — that's the only position the measurement is for.

★ Pro Tip

The bracket is not the bottom of the TV. The number in that chart is where the bottom edge of the screen ends up, but you drill for the wall plate — and the VESA holes are almost never centered on the back of the TV.

Lay the TV face down on a blanket, hook the arms onto it, and measure from the bottom edge of the TV up to the exact point where the bracket catches the wall plate. Add that measurement to your chart number. That's your drill line. Skipping this step is the most common way a carefully planned height ends up three inches off.

How We Actually Pick The Height On A Job

The chart gives you a target. On an actual install we work the problem from the opposite end — from the floor up — because the room has already made some decisions for you.

1. Start with what's already there

Before anything else we look at what's going underneath: a media console, a dresser, a floating shelf, a fireplace, a radiator, a baseboard heater. That furniture isn't moving for the sake of a measurement, so it sets the ground floor of the whole conversation.

2. Add what goes on top of it

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that changes the number most. It's never just the stand — it's everything that lives on the stand:

  • A soundbar, which is usually 3″ to 4″ tall and needs to sit in front of the screen without covering the bottom of the picture.
  • A center channel speaker, which is often taller than the soundbar people were picturing.
  • The cable box and game consoles, and whether the remote still needs line of sight to them.
  • Plants, framed photos, lamps, the decorative bowl — the things that are on there right now and are going straight back once we leave.

Measure the tallest of those, add breathing room above it, and you have the real answer to a question the charts never ask: what is the lowest the bottom of this TV can physically go in this room?

3. Compare the floor to the target

Now there are two numbers on the table — the lowest the TV can go, and where eye level says it should go. One of two things is true:

  • There's room to spare. The furniture allows the TV lower than eye level needs, so we mount at the eye-level number and everyone's happy.
  • The furniture wins. A tall console with a soundbar on it can push the lowest possible position above the eye-level target. Then that's the height, and the honest thing to say is that the stand is what decided it — not the wall, and not us.

4. Read the whole room, not just the couch

Everything above assumes one viewing position. In a lot of homes — especially open-plan ones — that assumption is simply false, and it's the caveat that matters most in a real layout.

People watch from all over the place. Standing at the kitchen island while cooking. Sitting at the dining table through dinner. Working at a desk off to one side of the room. In an open floor plan, one TV is often serving three areas at once, and the couch is just the one we all default to measuring from.

Those positions don't share a sightline. Standing at a counter puts eye level around 60″. A dining chair is higher than a sofa cushion. A desk off to the side is looking at the screen from an angle, not straight on. Mount purely for the couch and the TV can end up too low to see from the kitchen, or half-blocked by the back of the sectional from the dining table.

So we walk the room and stand in each of those spots before committing to a number. The goal isn't a perfect height for one chair — it's the height that works acceptably from every place people actually watch from. In practice that usually means landing a bit above the pure seated-eye-level number, trading a little comfort at the couch for a screen that's genuinely visible everywhere else.

When the positions are too far apart to reconcile, that's the clearest case there is for a full-motion mount: set the height for wherever people watch longest, and let the arm swing the screen toward the kitchen or the table when it's needed. It's also where a soundbar bracketed to the TV earns its keep, since the audio turns with the picture instead of staying aimed at an empty sofa.

5. The tape measure trick

Here's how we make the height real to someone before a single hole gets drilled, and it takes about thirty seconds.

Pull the tape measure out to the height of the TV — the actual screen height, so 32″ for a 65″ set — and hold it against the wall where the TV would go. Then ask the customer to sit down and picture their eyes landing in the middle of the tape.

That's it. People can't picture a screen from a number on a wall, but they can instantly tell you whether their eyes are in the middle of a thing they're looking at. Slide the tape up or down until it feels right, mark it, and you have a height everyone agrees on. It works about 99 times out of 100.

6. Then it's the customer's call

That lowest workable position is where we start the conversation, not where we end it. Plenty of people look at it and want the TV higher — for sightlines over a sectional, to clear a piece of art, or just because it looks better to them on the wall. That's fine. We move it up.

What we won't do is move it up without saying what it costs you. Every inch above eye level is an inch your neck pays for over a two-hour movie, and it's a lot easier to hear that with the tape measure still on the wall than six months later.

★ Pro Tip

Put the soundbar on the stand before you decide. Not the box it came in — the actual soundbar, in its actual spot.

A soundbar that turns out to clip the bottom two inches of the screen is the single most common reason a mount gets moved after the fact, and moving a mount means new holes in the wall and patching the old ones. Five minutes of staging beats an afternoon of drywall repair.

The soundbar doesn't have to sit on the stand at all

Worth knowing before you let a soundbar dictate your mounting height, because most people are working from two assumptions and both are wrong.

The usual belief is that a soundbar has exactly two homes: screwed to the wall, or sitting on the mantel or media stand. There's a third option almost nobody knows about — a universal soundbar mount, the kind Rocketfish makes, that brackets the soundbar directly to the TV itself and hangs it just below the screen.

Two things change when you do that:

  • The stand stops setting your minimum height. The tallest thing on the console was probably the soundbar. Take it off there and the TV is free to come down to where eye level actually wants it.
  • On a full-motion mount, the sound follows the screen. This is the real reason to do it. The soundbar is physically attached to the TV, so when the arm swings the screen toward the kitchen or the dining table, the audio swings with it. The sound is always pointed wherever the picture is pointed — instead of firing straight ahead into an empty couch while you're watching from somewhere else.

It also looks deliberate in a way a soundbar balanced on a shelf doesn't: one unit, one clean line under the screen, and one less thing on the console. If a soundbar is anywhere in your plans, mention it when you book — it changes the height conversation before it starts, not after the holes are drilled.

7. Get everyone who watches TV into the room

The tape measure trick works on the person standing in front of it. The remaining one time in a hundred is almost always the same story: the height gets agreed, the TV goes up, and somebody who lives there and wasn't home takes one look that evening and decides it's too high or too low.

Then it comes down, the holes get patched, and it goes back up somewhere else — which is a real afternoon of work to undo a decision that took thirty seconds to make.

So if two people share the room, get both of them in front of the tape measure before anything is drilled. It is the cheapest step in the entire job and the one that saves the most rework. If the other person genuinely can't be there, a photo of the tape against the wall with the furniture in frame settles it nearly as well.

The Fireplace Problem

This is the one that comes up on nearly every job, and it's worth being straight about.

Mounting above a mantel puts the screen roughly 60 to 70 inches off the floor. Against a 42″ target, that's not a small compromise — it's nearly two feet too high. It's also the single most requested TV location in Chicago homes, because the chimney breast is often the only uninterrupted wall in the room and the furniture is already pointed at it.

What actually happens afterward is predictable. The room looks great. Watching anything in it gets uncomfortable in about fifteen minutes, nobody ever quite names the reason, and the family drifts back to whichever room has the smaller TV at the right height.

A tilting mount is the usual suggested fix and it only half works. Tilt angles the picture down toward you, which genuinely helps with glare and with the washed-out look you get viewing a panel off-axis — but your neck is still tipped back. Tilt fixes the picture, not the posture.

The fix that does work is a pull-down mount that carries the TV down and forward — often 20 inches or more — so it sits at normal eye level while you're watching, then swings back flush when you're done. That's what a MantelMount is for, and it's the honest answer whenever someone wants the TV over the fireplace and also wants to enjoy watching it.

Worth knowing if your fireplace works: a lit fire pushes a column of hot air straight up the wall. A pull-down mount lets you bring the screen out of that column, which a fixed bracket can't do.

Where The Rule Bends

42″ is the living-room answer. A few rooms genuinely want something else.

  • Bedrooms — go higher. If you watch lying down or propped against a headboard, your eyes are lower but your sightline points up, which inverts the whole calculation. Most bedroom installs end up with the center of the screen between 48″ and 60″. Get in bed, have someone hold the TV up, and pick the height from there.
  • Kitchens and bars — go higher. Standing or perched on a stool raises eye level to 55″–65″, and these are screens you glance at rather than settle in with.
  • Low or modular seating — go lower. A deep low-slung sectional can drop seated eye level to 36″ or so. Measure, don't assume.
  • Kids' rooms and playrooms — go lower, and go full-motion. Small people sitting on the floor are looking up at everything already.
  • Open-plan rooms — split the difference. Covered in step 4 above, and it's the most common exception of all: when the kitchen, the dining table and the couch are all watching the same screen, the height that serves everyone sits above the couch-only number.
★ Pro Tip

In an unusual room, leave the outline up overnight. The tape measure trick settles a normal living room on the spot. A bedroom, a bar, or a room with two very different seating positions is worth more than thirty seconds — run painter's tape at the actual screen dimensions and live with it for a day.

While You're Measuring: How Far Away To Sit

Height's the one people get wrong, but distance is the other half of why a wall-mounted TV either feels like a movie theater or feels like a monitor.

For a 4K TV, sit at roughly 1 to 1.5 times the screen's diagonal — about 5 to 8 feet for a 65″, 6 to 9 feet for a 75″. That's closer than the old rule people remember, and the reason is simply that 4K resolution doesn't show you its pixel structure at those distances the way 1080p did.

The practical consequence: if your couch is already fixed where it is and the screen feels small, the answer is usually a bigger TV rather than a rearranged room. A larger screen filling more of your field of view is most of what makes a film feel cinematic at home — provided it's at the right height, which is where we came in.

The Short Version

  • Middle of the screen at seated eye level — about 42″ from the floor for a standard sofa.
  • Bottom of the TV: roughly 28″ for a 55″, 26″ for a 65″, 24″ for a 75″, 21″ for an 85″.
  • Bigger screens hang lower, not higher. The middle is what stays fixed.
  • Measure from the bottom edge of the TV to the bracket catch, and add it — the VESA plate is rarely centered.
  • Work up from the furniture: the stand plus the tallest thing on it — usually the soundbar — sets the lowest the TV can go.
  • Check every spot people watch from — kitchen, dining table, a desk off to the side — not just the couch. Open-plan rooms usually land a bit higher than the couch-only number.
  • Hold a tape measure out to the screen's height against the wall and have them picture their eyes in the middle of it. Works nearly every time.
  • A soundbar can bracket straight to the TV instead of the stand — frees the console, lets the TV drop, and on a full-motion arm the sound follows the screen.
  • Get everyone who shares the room in front of that tape before drilling. It's the cheapest step and it prevents the remount.
  • Above a fireplace is 60″–70″, far too high. Tilt fixes the picture, not your neck; a pull-down mount fixes both.
  • Bedrooms, kitchens and bars go higher. Low sectionals and kids' rooms go lower.
  • Tape the outline on the wall and sit with it for a day before anyone drills.
