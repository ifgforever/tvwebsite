HOW TO DRILL INTO TILE WITHOUT CRACKING IT

Tile is the hardest thing we drill, and the only wall we charge extra for. Here's what it actually takes — and the part that makes a mistake so expensive.

Four Rules, And Most Failures Break One Of Them

Cracked tile almost never comes down to bad luck. It comes down to one of four things, and if you get all four right the job is slow but genuinely predictable:

  1. Hammer mode off. This is the big one. More tiles are cracked by hammer action than by everything else combined.
  2. The right bit. Carbide for ordinary ceramic, diamond for porcelain and stone.
  3. Slow, with light steady pressure. Speed and force are what break tile. Patience is what gets through it.
  4. Keep it cool. Heat destroys the bit and stresses the tile. A little water changes everything.

That's the whole method. The rest of this explains why each one matters, because knowing the reason is what stops you improvising at the wrong moment.

Rule 1: Turn Hammer Mode Off

Every combi drill has a hammer setting, and on masonry it's genuinely useful — it works by hammering the bit forward thousands of times a minute while it spins, pulverising brick and concrete.

Tile is exactly the wrong material for that. It's hard but brittle, it's thin, and the face you're drilling is stretched over whatever's behind it. Pounding a brittle sheet is how you crack it. You'll usually chip the glaze on the way in and split the tile shortly after.

Switch to rotary — the plain drill-bit icon, no hammer symbol. Cutting through tile is done by grinding, not impact.

One exception, and it matters on Chicago walls: once you're all the way through the tile and into brick or concrete behind it, you can switch hammer mode back on for the rest of the hole. Through the tile, never.

Rule 2: The Right Bit — And Why Porcelain Is Different

“Tile” covers materials that behave nothing alike, and the one people get wrong is porcelain.

Porcelain is a ceramic, but it's made from finer clay fired hotter and denser. That makes it much harder and far less porous than standard glazed ceramic — which is exactly why it gets used on floors, in showers and increasingly on feature walls. It's also why a bit that sails through bathroom ceramic will sit on porcelain, polish a shiny spot, and go blunt.

If you can't tell what you have, assume porcelain and use a diamond bit. A diamond bit cuts ceramic perfectly well, so guessing that direction costs nothing. Guessing the other way costs you a bit and possibly a tile.

Worth knowing before you start: diamond bits are consumables. They dull, and hard tile dulls them quickly. That's not a sign of a cheap bit, it's what the tool is.

Rule 3: Slow, Steady, And Stop It Wandering

Glazed tile is slick, and a spinning bit skates across it — scratching a long arc across the glaze on its way to somewhere you didn't want a hole. That scratch is permanent.

Two fixes, and they stack:

  • A cross of masking or painter's tape over the spot. The tape gives the tip something to bite and stops it sliding. Mark your point on the tape rather than the tile.
  • Start at a slight angle, then come upright. With a spear-point bit, touch the edge of the tip down first to score a small notch, then bring the drill square once it's seated.

Then go slowly. Low speed, light pressure, and let the bit grind. Leaning on it doesn't speed it up — it overheats the tip and loads the tile.

★ Pro Tip

Ease off right at the breakthrough. The most likely moment to crack a tile is the instant the bit passes through the back face.

Up to then the tile is supported by its own thickness. At breakthrough the remaining material is thin, and if you're pushing hard the bit lurches forward and blows a chip out of the back — sometimes taking a crack with it. When you feel it start to give, lighten right off and let it finish itself.

Rule 4: Water

Grinding through hard tile makes real heat, fast. Heat is what kills diamond bits, and it puts thermal stress into a brittle material that's already being cut.

You don't need equipment. A damp sponge held under the hole, a spray bottle, or pausing to dip the bit is enough on a wall. Keep the tip wet and cool, and you get a cleaner hole and a bit that survives to do the next one.

Two related habits worth having: clear the dust out as you go so the bit isn't grinding its own debris, and stop periodically rather than drilling one continuous burn.

Where To Put The Hole

Position matters as much as technique.

Stay away from edges and corners. Aim for the middle of a tile where you can, and keep roughly an inch clear of any edge. Tile is weakest near its perimeter, and a hole close to the edge has far less material resisting the crack.

Grout lines are more forgiving, with a catch. Grout is softer and drills easier, and a damaged grout line can be raked out and repointed — a damaged tile can't. But mounting holes have to land where the anchors go, and grout lines almost never fall exactly there. Never move a mount's anchor points to chase a grout line. The anchor position is what carries your TV; that decision is made by the framing behind the wall, not by the tile pattern in front of it.

Listen for hollow spots. Tap around before you drill. If the thinset behind a tile didn't fully bond, there's a void, and a tile bridging a void has nothing behind it when the bit breaks through. Those are the ones that blow out. A hollow-sounding tile is a good reason to shift position if the anchor layout allows.

★ Pro Tip

The tile holds nothing. What's behind it does. Tile is a finish, not structure — it carries none of your TV's weight.

Getting through the tile is stage one of the hole. Stage two is whatever's behind: studs, cement board, plaster, brick. Swap to the correct bit for that material and choose the anchor for it. On steel framing that means heavy-duty toggles rather than the lag bolts in the box — which is its own subject.

The Real Risk Isn't The Hole

Here's the part that doesn't come up until it's too late, and it's the reason we treat tile so carefully.

If you crack a tile, the problem usually isn't replacing it. It's finding one that matches.

Tile lines get discontinued constantly. Dye lots shift between production runs, so even the same product from a different batch can sit slightly off against its neighbours in daylight. If the tile went up more than a couple of years ago, the odds of buying a matching replacement off a shelf are not good.

Which means a cracked tile in the middle of a feature wall or a backsplash isn't a small repair. It's a visible flaw in a finished surface, permanently, unless someone happens to have kept spares in the basement — and most people didn't.

If you have leftover tiles from the original install, find them before anyone drills. Knowing a spare exists changes the whole risk calculation, and it's worth five minutes in the storage unit.

The Short Version

  • Hammer mode off through the tile. Back on once you're into brick behind it.
  • Carbide for glazed ceramic; diamond for porcelain, stone and glass. Unsure? Use diamond.
  • Tape a cross over the spot so the bit can't skate and scratch the glaze.
  • Low speed, light pressure, and ease right off at breakthrough — that's when it cracks.
  • Keep the tip wet. Heat kills diamond bits and stresses the tile.
  • Middle of the tile, an inch clear of edges. Tap for hollow spots first.
  • Never move anchor points to chase grout lines. The framing decides where the holes go.
  • Tile holds nothing — the anchor is chosen for what's behind it.
  • The real risk is a discontinued tile you can't match. Find your spares before drilling.
