FLOATING TV STANDS: HOW TO PICK ONE THAT ACTUALLY HIDES THE CORDS

A floating console is the single best-looking thing you can put under a wall-mounted TV. It's also the easiest one to buy wrong.

Why Floating Stands Look So Good

Nothing changes a living room faster than getting the furniture off the floor. A floating TV stand — sometimes called a wall-mounted console, a floating media cabinet, or a levitating credenza — bolts straight into the wall with nothing underneath it. The result is a room that reads bigger than it is.

Three reasons it works so well:

  • The floor stays visible. Uninterrupted flooring under the console tricks the eye into reading more square footage. In a Chicago condo or a tight bungalow living room, that's free space you didn't have to renovate for.
  • It cleans up in ten seconds. No legs, no dust trap underneath, no dragging a 90-pound cabinet out to vacuum behind it.
  • It lines up with the TV. A wall-mounted TV over a floor-standing cabinet is two pieces of furniture. A wall-mounted TV over a floating console is one composition — one horizontal line, centered, deliberate.

That's the promise. Here's where people get burned.

The Catalog Photo Is Lying To You

Open any furniture catalog and look at the floating stands. The gorgeous ones are almost always open-shelf designs: a slab of oak with two cubbies, maybe a lower shelf, everything visible. In the photo it holds a small plant, three hardcover books, and a decorative bowl.

Notice what it never holds in that photo: a cable box, a game console, a streaming stick, a soundbar, a power strip, and the eight cords those things come with.

That styling shot is not showing you a media console. It's showing you a shelf. The moment you put your actual equipment on it, an open floating stand becomes a floating display case for wires — and because it's mounted at eye level with nothing behind it, every one of those cords is backlit against a bare wall. It looks worse than the cabinet you were trying to replace.

★ Pro Tip

Buy the one with the drawers, not the one with the shelves. If it doesn't have doors or drawers, it has nowhere for your gear to live — and every cable will be on permanent display.

What you actually want is a floating console with closed storage and a hidden compartment behind it: drawers or flip-down doors on the front, and a hollow back with a cable pass-through cut into the panel. Your cable box, router, console, and power strip all go inside. The cords run out the back cutout, straight down the wall (or into it), and disappear.

Before you click buy, look for these four things in the product photos and dimensions:

  • A cable pass-through or open back. A sealed box with no hole is a coffin for your equipment. If the listing doesn't show the back panel, assume there isn't one worth having.
  • Real interior depth — 14″ or more. Plenty of floating consoles are only 9″ deep, which is too shallow for a cable box plus the cord bend behind it. Measure your deepest component and add three inches.
  • IR-friendly doors, or a plan. Solid wood doors block your remote. Look for smoked-glass or fabric-front doors, or budget for an IR repeater if you're hiding the box completely.
  • Ventilation. An enclosed compartment with a receiver in it needs airflow. A slotted back or a gap at the top is the difference between a hidden component and a cooked one.

The design language to search for is “floating TV console with hidden storage,” “wall-mounted media cabinet with drawers,” or “floating credenza with cable management.” If a listing only says “floating shelf,” keep scrolling.

The Part Nobody Mentions: What's Behind The Wall

A floating console is cantilevered furniture. Everything it holds — plus the leverage of hanging out from the wall — lands on whatever you anchored it to.

Into wood studs with proper lag bolts, a floating console will hold a full stack of components without flinching. Into drywall anchors alone, it will sag within a year and eventually pull out and take a chunk of wall with it. That's the whole story, and it's why the mounting matters more than the brand.

Chicago walls make this more interesting than the instruction sheet assumes:

  • Plaster over lath in older two-flats and greystones — the finder reads solid everywhere and the anchors bite into nothing. You need to find real framing.
  • Brick and concrete in loft conversions and condo demising walls — different hardware entirely, and the cable path can't go through the wall, so it has to be planned around it.
  • Studs that don't line up with where you want the console centered. That's a solvable problem with a backing cleat, but it's the reason a lot of DIY installs end up two inches off-center.
★ Pro Tip

Hang the console first, then the TV. The console's height sets everything else. Get it on the wall, load it with your actual gear, sit on your actual couch — then mark the TV.

Leave 6″ to 12″ between the top of the console and the bottom of the TV. Tighter than that and the two pieces crowd each other; wider and they stop reading as one unit. That gap is also where a soundbar lives, so if a soundbar is anywhere in your future, plan for it now instead of re-drilling later.

Finishing The Look: Get The Cords Into The Wall

A closed console hides the equipment. It doesn't, by itself, hide the run between the console and the TV — and that six-to-twelve-inch stretch of exposed HDMI and power cable is exactly what ruins the effect.

Two ways to close the gap:

  • In-wall concealment — the clean version. A low-voltage pass-through behind the TV, a second one behind the console, and the cables run inside the wall between them. Power gets relocated properly with an in-wall-rated kit, not an extension cord fished through drywall.
  • Paintable raceway — the renter-friendly version. A slim channel over the drywall, painted to match. Not invisible, but at ten feet it disappears, and it comes off without patching.

That's the work we do on almost every floating-console job in the Chicago area: mount the console into real framing, mount the TV above it at the right height, and get every visible cable out of sight so the setup looks the way it did in the photo that sold you on it — only this time with your equipment actually in it.

The Short Version

  • Floating stands look great because the floor stays visible — the room reads bigger.
  • Open-shelf floating stands hide nothing. Catalog photos stage them empty on purpose.
  • Buy closed storage: drawers or doors, a hidden compartment, a cable pass-through, 14″+ depth, ventilation, and an IR plan.
  • Anchor into framing, not drywall. Plaster, brick, and concrete each need different hardware.
  • Console first, TV second, 6″–12″ of air between them.
  • Run the last stretch of cable in-wall or in a painted raceway, or the whole effect falls apart.
