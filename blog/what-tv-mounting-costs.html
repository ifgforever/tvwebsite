HOW MUCH DOES IT COST TO MOUNT A TV? WHAT'S ACTUALLY PUBLISHED

We went looking for what Chicago installers charge. Ten of the fifteen we checked don't publish a price at all — and of the five that do, every single one says “starting at.”

The Short Answer

For labor alone, most TV mounting in the United States lands between $100 and $350. The averages depend on who you ask, and they don't agree:

SourceWhat they report
Thumbtack$139 average, $120–150 typical
Geek Squad$99.99–$249.99 in-home, plus a $25 weekend/holiday surcharge
TaskRabbit (Chicago)$150–350, with each tasker setting their own rate
Angi$249 average, $150–348 range; wiring or fireplace work adds $150–300

That's a wide spread for the same task, and the reason is simple: those numbers are measuring different scopes. Some include the bracket, most don't. Some include hiding the cables, most don't. Comparing them straight across tells you almost nothing.

Every figure on this page was taken from a public source in August 2026. Prices move — check before you rely on any of them, including ours.

Where The Money Actually Goes

Base price is the part everyone competes on, so it's the part that stays low. The bill grows in the add-ons, and those are far less consistent from company to company.

Screen size

Size tiers are the most common structure in the trade, and the spread is steep — among Chicago companies that publish, the same job on an 85″ can run more than double what it runs on a 50″. A big TV genuinely is heavier and wants a second pair of hands. But what really decides how long a mount takes is the wall, not the screen: an 85″ on modern wood studs is a faster job than a 43″ on century-old plaster. That's why we don't charge by size.

The mount itself

Read the fine print for “customer supplied parts.” A base price that excludes the bracket isn't comparable to one that includes it, and brackets range from $30 to several hundred. Ours are $50 for fixed or tilting and $100 for full motion, listed openly.

Hiding the wires

This is the widest spread of anything we found: in-wall concealment is published anywhere from $59 to $175, and a recessed outlet behind the screen runs up to $299. Nationally Angi puts concealed wiring, soundbar brackets and fireplace work at $150–300 on top. If cables matter to you, ask about this before you compare base prices at all — it can be the largest line on the invoice.

The wall

Plaster, brick, concrete and steel studs all take longer than drywall, and many installers charge a “difficult wall” surcharge for them. We don't — those are normal Chicago walls. Tile is our one exception, at $50, because it genuinely destroys drill bits.

★ Pro Tip

Get every quote to price the same five things. Otherwise you're comparing numbers that aren't measuring the same job.

Ask each one: the base price for your screen size; whether the bracket is included; the cost to hide the cables the way you want them hidden; anything extra for your wall type; and whether there's a surcharge for evenings or weekends. The cheapest headline number frequently isn't the cheapest total, and you can't tell which until all five are on the table.

Why Booking Through A Lead Site Costs More

Look again at those national averages. Thumbtack reports $139. Angi reports $249 — nearly double, for the same task. That gap isn't about quality, and it isn't a coincidence. It's the business model.

Sites like Angi and Thumbtack don't take a cut of the job. They sell leads — your contact details, to contractors, up front, whether or not anyone ever books you:

  • Angi charges roughly $15 to $85 per lead (sometimes over $100), on top of a membership that runs around $300 a year. Each enquiry is sold to three to eight businesses at once.
  • Thumbtack runs $25 to $75 per lead for most home-service trades, with each one shared with four or five other pros.

Now do the arithmetic from the contractor's side. Five installers each pay for the same enquiry. One of them gets the job. The other four paid for nothing — and roughly three quarters of homeowners simply hire whoever replies first, so it isn't even the best installer who wins, it's the fastest one to a phone.

At $25 a lead and one win in five, the installer who does get your job has spent about $125 acquiring it. At $50 a lead, $250. That money is real and it has to come from somewhere, so it comes from the price on the invoice — yours, and everyone else's who booked through the same channel.

To be fair to the platforms: they do deliver customers, and that service is worth paying for if you have no other way to find work. But you should understand what you're looking at when a lead-site quote comes in higher. You are paying for the four quotes that didn't win.

★ Pro Tip

Once you've shortlisted someone, book them directly. Use the lead sites to find candidates and read reviews — that's what they're genuinely good at.

Then go to the installer's own website or phone number for the actual booking. Same person, same work, without the acquisition cost baked into your quote. It also means your details get sold to one business instead of five, which is why booking direct usually ends the flood of calls rather than starting it.

What About The Guarantee?

There's a genuine reason people book through the platforms, and it isn't price — it's protection. For a stranger drilling into your wall, that's a reasonable thing to want. But the guarantees are not equivalent, and the gap between them is enormous.

Thumbtack GuaranteeAngi Happiness Guarantee

Money backup to $1,000up to the purchase price
Requires paying on-platformYesYes
Versus your own insurance—Yours is primary; this is secondary

Forty times the coverage, for what a homeowner experiences as the same act of booking a tradesperson through an app. Almost nobody comparing the two knows that.

And Thumbtack's — the stronger of the two — is narrower than most people assume:

  • You have to pay through the platform. Pay your installer in cash, by check, or through Venmo — the way most people actually pay a tradesperson — and the guarantee is void.
  • It excludes faulty installation and incomplete work. It covers direct physical damage, not the job being done badly. For TV mounting specifically, that's a meaningful gap: a bad install is the most likely thing to go wrong.
  • The clock is short. Fourteen days to report property damage. A mount that was anchored wrong often doesn't fail for months — that's exactly how anchors fail, slowly — and by then the window has closed.

So it's real protection, with real edges. What it is not is insurance on the installer.

It's also worth being honest about how rarely any of this gets used. For a job this size, the thing that actually keeps installers honest isn't a claims process — it's that the work is public. A company that doesn't take care of people accumulates reviews saying so, on Google and Yelp, where the next customer reads them. That record follows a local installer far more closely than a guarantee they'll probably never claim against, and it's the single most useful thing you can check before booking anyone.

★ Pro Tip

The direct-booking equivalent is simply asking whether the installer is insured — and asking for proof.

Any legitimate contractor carries commercial general liability insurance and can produce a certificate naming you, your landlord, or your building. That's a document from an insurance carrier, not a platform policy, and it doesn't care how you paid or whether you reported inside a fortnight.

We're insured and we'll send you a certificate on request — ask and we'll have our agent issue one made out to whoever needs it. Chicago buildings ask for these constantly before they'll let anyone work in the building, so it's a routine request, not an awkward one. If an installer can't produce one, that tells you something on its own.

The Cost That Never Appears On The Invoice

There's a difference between a big installer and an owner-operator that nobody prices, and it shows up on the day rather than on the quote.

You don't know who's coming

When you book a large company, you're booking a brand. The rating you read is an average across dozens of technicians, and the person who knocks on your door isn't named in any of those reviews. They might be excellent. You have no way to know, because nothing you read was about them.

Book an owner-operator and the reviews and the person are the same thing. Whoever is at your door is the one whose name is on the record and who wears every job. That alignment is the whole value of a review, and it quietly disappears the moment the company is big enough to have staff.

The installer often isn't allowed to solve your problem

This is the one that costs customers the most, and it's rarely the technician's fault.

A tech working for a big-box retailer or a large outfit works to a scope sheet. There's a standard install, a list of what's included, and a list of what they're not permitted to touch. When your room needs something slightly off that script — a different height because of where the sectional actually sits, drilling a surface the policy excludes, a second trip to the van for a bracket that suits your wall better, twenty more minutes to get the tilt exactly right — a policy-bound installer frequently can't do it. Not won't. Can't. The answer is "that's not something we do," and the job ends at the boundary of the scope sheet rather than at the point where your setup is actually right.

Someone who owns the business makes that call standing in your living room. If the job needs another twenty minutes, it gets another twenty minutes. If it needs a different approach than the one planned on the phone, it gets one. There's nobody to ask.

Two examples we run into constantly, both of which end with a customer being told no:

“You'll need to speak to a tile expert”

Big-box installers routinely decline tile. The customer books a TV mount, the tech arrives, sees a tile wall or a tiled fireplace surround, and the job stops there — go find a tile specialist.

Tile is genuinely the hardest surface we drill, and it's the one wall we charge extra for. But it's a normal part of the work, not a different trade. Hammer mode off, a diamond bit, slow and wet, and ease off at breakthrough — it's a slower job, at $50 more, and it gets done on the same visit. Nobody needs to be referred anywhere.

“It has to go on the stud, so it can't be centered”

This one comes up on Samsung Frame TVs, and it's worth understanding because the customer usually loses the argument.

The Frame uses an unusual flush mount with two mounting points, and the aim is to catch at least one stud across them. When the studs don't line up with where the customer actually wants the TV — centered on the wall, centered over the fireplace, centered on the console — a policy-bound installer's answer is that the TV moves. So a television bought specifically to look like a framed picture ends up hung visibly off-center, because of where a stud happens to be.

Where a stud genuinely isn't available, we use heavy-duty snap toggles in place of it. The load case here is about as gentle as wall mounting gets: a Frame is a light television, and it sits nearly flush, so there's almost no leverage pulling the anchors out of the wall — nothing like the cantilever of a full-motion arm holding an 85″ screen twenty inches off the drywall. Those toggles are rated in the hundreds of pounds each, far beyond what a Frame asks of them, and we've never had one come back.

The result is the TV hangs where the customer wanted it. That isn't a trick or a shortcut — it's just being allowed to choose the right hardware for the situation instead of following a rule written for every situation at once.

★ Pro Tip

Two questions worth asking before you book anyone:

“Who will actually be doing the install?” and “If my setup needs something a bit different on the day, can the person here decide that, or does it need approval?”

Neither is a hostile question and both are answered instantly. The answers tell you more about how your afternoon will go than any price on a quote.

To be fair about the trade-off: a big company has real advantages. Scheduling capacity, someone to send if your installer is sick, a corporate complaints process. If those matter most to you, that's a legitimate reason to choose one. Just know you're trading away the flexibility, and that the trade isn't listed anywhere on the price sheet.
